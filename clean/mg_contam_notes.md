# Potential contamination in MG samples

## Observations
 - General level of contamination is very low as indicated by control samples (see output from `contam_mg.R` and `contam_se.R`).
 - Shared ASVs are 20 times more likely to come from positive controls than from other SE ASVs (computed on sets of unique ASVs)
 - We have more contamination of MG litter samples with SE taxa, clearly above what is acceptable. There is a little bit of contamination of lysate samples too, but at very low levels. The contamination occurs across samples in both datasets; it is not a problem of mislabeled samples (we had an instance of this before).
 - It is challenging to identify problem clusters but one method is to focus on hexapod clusters that share at least one ASV with Swedish IBA data from 2019; they clearly would appear problematic. A number of these “problem” clusters actually appear to be shared fungal sequences that are incorrectly annotated by sintax to unclassified.Insecta at the order level. I assume that fungal CO1 sequences simply are very conservative. Let us remove those from the set of problem clusters.
 - For the following items, see output from the script `filter_options.R`.
 - There are then 1266 problem clusters for MG litter samples and 159 clusters for MG lysate samples.
 - Our previous approach of filtering out control clusters based on 5% frequency for all MG samples was bad for litter samples. It leaves 1253 of the 1266 problem clusters. However, if we apply the criterion to litter samples and lysate samples separately, 1/3 of the problem clusters disappear from the litter data (829 problem clusters left). There is almost no difference for lysate samples (147 vs 152 for separate application). If we apply the criterion separately, we can also remove all clusters in control samples; it makes no difference for litter samples (still 829 problem clusters) and almost no difference for lysate samples (now down to 149 problem clusters from 152).
 - The contamination level measured in this way is 1.0% of clusters and each problem occurs in an average of 1% of samples and an average of 30 reads in MG litter data. For lysate data it is 0.098% of clusters, 0.3% of samples and an average of 200 reads. The large mean number of reads for lysate “problem” clusters  is probably due to the fact that some of these clusters are actually authentic signal.
 - I went through the 10 “problem” clusters occurring in the largest number of samples for litter and lysate data, respectively. Most lysate clusters on this list seem plausible global taxa (no clear example of a contaminant), most of the litter clusters seem likely to be contamination from Swedish taxa.
 - The "problem" clusters are found in the files `mg_litter_problemclusters_wo_controls.tsv` and `mg_lysate_problemclusters_wo_controls.tsv`, with data on reads, samples and taxonomic annotation; they are created by the `filter_options.R` script. More exhaustive research on the clusters can be made using these files.
 - Removing also clusters that occur in positive controls in litter and lysate taxa, reduces “problem” clusters to 624 and 94, respectively. Among the removed “problem” clusters in lysate data, I found one likely contaminant but the other clusters I looked at (around ten) seemed plausible authentic signal. Around half of the “problem” clusters shared between samples and positive controls in the litter data did not have the expected read ratios (they should be considerably higher in positive controls) consistent with contamination from positive controls (but the other half did). Among the 624 remaining “problem” clusters in litter data, most still appear to be contaminants from Swedish taxa.
 - Increasing the abundance filtering does not help much in reducing the contamination problem in the litter data. For instance, removing all clusters with up to 50 max reads reduces the "problem" clusters to 214 (from 829) but at the same time only leaves 23% of the litter clusters after filtering.

## Conclusions and notes
 - There is an unacceptable level of contamination in the published MG litter data. It comes partly from positive controls and partly from other sources of DNA from Swedish taxa, possibly from PCR amplification of pre-SIMS samples at Station Linné or from individual-specimen barcoding at Station Linné. Some of it may come from the BSA added to the litter samples but it seems relatively unlikely as a major source of contamination.
 - Control clusters should be removed separately for the litter and lysate data (and updated versions of these datasets should be published). The effect on the lysate data will be negligible, while the litter data will improve notably in quality.
 - Filtering out clusters from the positive controls is bad practice and does not help much in reducing the problem for litter data and it probably removes more authentic signal than noise from the lysate data.
 - During the manual exploration of some clusters with strange distributions, we discovered 80 MG clusters annotated as "Rhadalidae" and assigned to `BOLD:AEC4752` (see notes in the file `contamination_analysis.html`. The sequences BLAST to bacterial sequences. This spurious signal is apparently caused by bacterial sequences matching an incorrect BOLD BIN. This BOLD BIN has since been removed from the BOLD database.
 - Interestingly, the EPA_NG annotation for the 216 ASVs with this BOLD assignment was usually Reclinomonas americana (141), which would seem somewhat appropriate, but Fujientomon dicestum was also common (64). This sequence actually BLASTs to fungal sequences (and onto itself), so it is likely to be an error and should be removed from the Chesters reference tree.
